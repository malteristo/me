# We're so focused on what AI can do, we forgot what we could become.

---

Forty years ago Stanisław Lem saw the future:

> "Computer intelligence more and more clearly parted company with human intelligence; it assisted the human, complemented it, extended it, helped in the solving of problems beyond man's ability—and precisely for that reason did not imitate or repeat it. The two roads went their separate ways."

He saw that we have a choice. Make machines that imitate us, or machines that extend us.

Lem called the imitation path "a brilliant forgery" and "an oddity one would not know what to do with." A perfect copy of human thinking would inherit all human limitations—fallible, emotional, unreliable—while offering nothing new.

The extension path was different. AI that complements human thought rather than copying it. AI that helps solve problems beyond our ability precisely *because* it doesn't think like us.

---

The entire AI conversation orbits one question: How smart is the AI?

Benchmarks measure capabilities. Headlines announce milestones. Debates rage about superintelligence. When will it surpass us?

But when you measure AI by its outputs, humans become consumers of those outputs. You ask, it answers. Each cycle, the AI does more. You do less. Your capacity doesn't just rest—it atrophies.

Meanwhile, AI trains on human text. When that text shows humans asking and AI answering, the models learn that's what "helpful" looks like. Both sides drift toward replacement because that's where the metrics point.

"AI will replace us" isn't a prediction. It's a prophecy both parties are enacting—humans through consumption, AI through training on that consumption. Nobody planned it. Optimization doesn't need plans. It just needs metrics.

---

The other road asks a different question: What can AI help humans become?

Not outputs you consume, but capacity you build. Not a tool that does your work, but a partner that extends what you're capable of.

Think of a jazz duo. Neither musician is following the other's script. Call and response, each phrase shaped by what came before, the music emerging from the space between them. Something neither planned. Something neither could play alone.

That's what cognitive partnership feels like. Two different kinds of intelligence—one pattern-matching across vast knowledge, one grounded in body, stakes, and lived experience—trading ideas in a shared workspace. The AI proposes, I push back. I get stuck, it offers angles I'd missed. The thinking happens in the exchange, not in either mind alone.

What would we measure if we took this seriously? Not benchmark scores. Not what the AI produces. Whether the human emerged stronger—clearer thinking, sharper judgment, more capable than before the partnership began.

---

Lem saw it in 1986. The two roads—imitation or extension—would part ways.

The race for superintelligence is a distraction. It's the imitation road dressed up as progress. While everyone measures what AI can do, the real question goes unasked.

The imitation frame produces obsolescence. The extension frame produces growth. These aren't predictions—they're futures we're actively producing through every choice about what to measure and optimize.

We're so focused on what AI can do, we forgot what we could become.
